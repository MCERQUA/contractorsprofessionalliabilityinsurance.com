Selecting the right limits and deductible structure for a contractors professional liability policy is one of the more consequential decisions a GC's risk management program makes each year — and one of the least systematically approached. Many firms anchor their limits to what a specific contract required last year, or match whatever their broker recommended at inception without revisiting the decision as the business grows. Neither approach is adequate.

The correct framework for sizing E&O limits integrates project exposure analysis, contract-mandated minimums, aggregate vs. per-claim structure, deductible capacity, and the reality — which many GCs misunderstand — of how umbrella and excess policies interact (or more precisely, fail to interact) with professional liability.

## Starting with Exposure Analysis

Limits selection begins with an honest appraisal of the professional services exposure your firm actually carries. Two GCs with identical annual revenue can have dramatically different professional liability exposure depending on their project mix.

A GC operating primarily under design-bid-build contracts, building to fully specified construction documents prepared by an owner-engaged architect, carries relatively modest professional liability exposure. The professional risk largely rides with the design team. The GC's professional exposure is concentrated in coordination, cost estimating, and scheduling.

A GC who operates primarily under design-build contracts, CM-at-risk agreements, or owner's representative arrangements carries substantially larger professional exposure because the professional services scope is broader and the professional responsibility is more directly assigned to the GC.

Exposure analysis should also consider project size and duration. A single design-build hospital project at $80 million generates more professional liability exposure than twelve smaller renovation projects. Professional errors on large, complex projects produce proportionally larger damages because the downstream consequences of a design coordination error, specification failure, or cost estimation mistake compound across larger scopes and longer schedules.

## Contract-Required Minimums

Most sophisticated owners — public agencies, institutional healthcare systems, large commercial developers — specify professional liability limits in their contract documents. These requirements set a floor, not an optimal level.

Common contract-mandated minimums by project type:

- Commercial office or retail under $10 million: $1 million per claim / $1 million aggregate
- Public work and institutional projects $10–$50 million: $1 million to $2 million per claim
- Healthcare, educational facilities, or complex institutional work: $2 million to $5 million per claim
- Major infrastructure or large-scale design-build: $5 million per claim or higher

A GC who carries $1 million per claim because that is what most contracts require may be underinsured if the firm's project mix includes a single large design-build project that could generate a professional claim materially exceeding that limit. Contract minimums protect the project owner's interest in recovery; they are not calibrated to protect the GC's financial position.

## Per-Claim vs. Aggregate: Understanding the Distinction

Most contractors professional liability policies are structured with two limits: a per-claim limit and an aggregate limit.

The **per-claim limit** is the maximum the insurer will pay for any single claim, including defense costs (if defense is within limits) and indemnification. A $2 million per-claim limit means no single claim can draw more than $2 million from the policy.

The **aggregate limit** is the maximum the insurer will pay for all claims during the policy period. An annual policy with a $2 million per-claim / $4 million aggregate will cover up to $4 million in total claims across the policy year, with no single claim exceeding $2 million.

The relationship between per-claim and aggregate determines how the policy responds to multiple claims in a policy year. A GC who carries a $1 million per-claim / $1 million aggregate policy — sometimes structured as $1M/$1M — has no additional aggregate capacity after the first claim exhausts the per-claim limit. The per-claim and aggregate limits are the same number, meaning one fully litigated claim can exhaust the policy entirely.

Contractors with diversified project portfolios — and particularly those with multiple concurrent design-build or CM-at-risk projects — should evaluate whether their aggregate limit adequately reflects the possibility of multiple claims in a single policy year. A $2M/$4M structure, for instance, provides twice the aggregate capacity while keeping the per-claim limit at $2 million.

## Deductible Structures: Flat Deductible vs. Self-Insured Retention

Professional liability policies use two fundamentally different deductible structures, and the distinction has material operational and financial consequences.

**Flat deductible:** The insurer defends and manages the claim from inception. The insured pays the deductible amount at resolution — typically applied to indemnity payments, defense costs, or both, depending on policy language. The insurer controls the defense throughout.

**Self-insured retention (SIR):** The insured is responsible for managing and paying all costs — defense and indemnity — up to the SIR amount. The insurer's obligations do not attach until the SIR is exhausted. This means the insured must engage its own counsel, manage the defense, and fund all costs within the retention before the insurer becomes involved.

SIR structures are generally used by larger, more sophisticated organizations with in-house risk management capabilities and sufficient cash flow to fund defense costs within the retention. For most GC principals without dedicated risk management staff, a flat deductible structure is operationally more practical. The insurer manages the defense from day one; the insured simply pays the deductible at the back end.

The tradeoff: SIR structures typically command lower premiums (because the insured absorbs more first-dollar risk), while flat deductible policies are more expensive but provide earlier insurer engagement. For a GC without the internal infrastructure to manage a complex professional liability defense, the lower premium of an SIR structure is frequently not worth the operational burden.

**Application of deductibles to defense costs** varies by policy. Some deductibles apply only to indemnity payments; others apply to the combined total of defense costs and indemnity. If a policy has a $50,000 deductible that applies to defense costs, and the defense of a frivolous claim costs $60,000 before dismissal, the GC pays $50,000 despite winning the case. Understanding how your deductible applies to defense costs is essential — particularly given how expensive E&O defense has become.

## Umbrella and Excess Policies: The Critical Misunderstanding

Many GCs assume that their umbrella or excess liability policy provides additional limits above their E&O policy when professional liability claims exhaust the primary limit. This assumption is almost universally incorrect.

Standard commercial umbrella policies — and most excess liability policies — include a professional liability exclusion. The umbrella will not drop down to cover professional negligence claims, and it will not sit as an additional layer of limit above the primary E&O policy. The exclusion is broadly worded and consistently enforced.

A GC with a $1 million E&O primary policy and a $10 million umbrella has exactly $1 million of protection for professional negligence claims. The umbrella provides zero additional protection.

The implication is direct: the E&O policy's own limits must be adequate to cover the full range of professional liability exposure the firm carries. There is no umbrella backstop. A GC whose business has grown significantly — in project size, project complexity, or professional services scope — but who has not increased E&O limits proportionally is operating with a protection gap that an umbrella will not fill.

Some carriers offer professional liability excess policies that sit above a primary E&O policy. These are not umbrella policies — they are excess E&O policies specifically designed to provide additional layers of professional liability coverage. For GCs on large design-build projects where a single professional claim could materially exceed the primary policy limits, excess E&O deserves consideration.

## Project-Specific vs. Annual Policy Considerations

Annual contractors professional liability policies cover all professional services the GC renders during the policy period, subject to the aggregate limit. All projects share the same aggregate.

Project-specific professional liability policies provide dedicated limits for a single project. The limits are not shared with other projects; a claim on Project A does not erode limits available for Project B.

Project-specific policies are appropriate when:

- A single project's professional liability exposure is large enough that an adverse claim could exhaust the annual policy's aggregate
- An owner contractually requires project-specific limits (common on large healthcare, infrastructure, and institutional projects)
- The GC is entering a high-complexity design-build project with professional liability exposure materially larger than the rest of the firm's portfolio

Project-specific policies are priced based on the project's professional services scope, duration, and limit. They typically cannot be canceled mid-project and provide an extended tail covering claims that arise post-completion — a meaningful advantage given that design and coordination errors often surface after substantial completion.

## Working with a Broker to Right-Size Coverage

The most effective approach to E&O limits selection is an annual structured conversation with a broker who specializes in construction professional liability — not a generalist commercial lines broker who places E&O as a secondary line.

That conversation should cover: a review of the prior year's project portfolio by professional services type and value, upcoming projects with significant professional liability exposure, contract minimums across the active book of work, the current deductible's effect on the firm's claims behavior, and whether the aggregate limit reflects realistic multi-claim scenarios.

Limits selection is not a set-it-and-forget-it decision. A firm that purchases $1M/$2M limits when it has $15 million in design-build revenue and later grows to $60 million in design-build revenue without revisiting limits has allowed its protection to erode in real terms even as its exposure has grown substantially.

---

The question of how much E&O coverage is enough has a different answer every year as your project mix, revenue, and professional services scope evolve. The firms that get this right are the ones that treat it as an annual discipline — not a one-time purchase decision.
