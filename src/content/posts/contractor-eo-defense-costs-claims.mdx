Receiving a professional liability claim is not the moment to learn how your E&O policy works. By the time a demand letter arrives — or worse, a summons — the strategic decisions that most affect outcome have already been made or missed. Prompt notice, understanding your insurer's defense cost structure, knowing what a reservation of rights letter means, and managing the insurer relationship through litigation all determine whether your policy actually performs as expected.

This guide addresses the mechanics of an E&O claim from first notice through resolution, with emphasis on the decisions and pitfalls that most affect GC principals.

## How Claims-Made E&O Policies Respond

Contractors professional liability is written on a claims-made basis. The policy responds when two conditions are met: a claim must be made against the insured, and that claim must be reported to the insurer — both during the policy period (or within an applicable extended reporting period).

A "claim" is typically defined in the policy as a written demand for money or services, a civil proceeding commenced by service of a complaint, or in some policy forms, a written notice of circumstances that might give rise to a claim. The breadth of the "claim" definition matters when a GC receives a demand letter that stops short of filing suit. Read your policy's definition carefully — in many forms, a written demand letter qualifies as a claim and starts the reporting clock.

The reporting obligation is not merely a formality. Failure to report a claim promptly — or failure to report a known circumstance that later develops into a claim — can give the insurer grounds to deny coverage. Many policies require notice "as soon as practicable" or "within 30/60/90 days" of a claim or potential claim. That language is enforced.

## Defense Within Limits vs. Defense Outside Limits

This is among the most consequential structural features of an E&O policy, and it is frequently misread or overlooked during policy selection.

**Defense within limits** (also called "eroding limits" or "burning limits") means that defense costs — attorney fees, expert witness costs, deposition costs, court filing costs — are paid from the same limit of liability that covers indemnity (the settlement or judgment). If your policy has a $1 million per-claim limit and defense costs run to $400,000, only $600,000 remains available for indemnification if the claim is resolved against you.

**Defense outside limits** (also called "non-eroding limits") means that defense costs are paid in addition to the limit of liability. A $1 million per-claim limit with defense outside limits provides $1 million for indemnification even if the defense cost substantially exceeds that amount.

The practical difference is significant on complex professional liability matters where defense costs commonly run from $200,000 to $700,000 before trial. A GC with a $1 million within-limits policy who faces a $900,000 demand may find — after $350,000 in defense costs — that the insurer has strong economic incentive to settle for $650,000 or less, regardless of the merits. The policy's erosion dynamic drives settlement strategy as much as the underlying facts.

When comparing E&O policies, the difference in premium between defense-within-limits and defense-outside-limits structures is real but often modest relative to the protection differential. Most risk managers and knowledgeable brokers recommend defense-outside-limits for GCs with significant project exposure.

## Reservation of Rights Letters

A reservation of rights (ROR) letter is the insurer's written notification that it will defend the claim while reserving its right to later deny coverage for some or all of the claim based on identified policy provisions. Receiving an ROR letter does not mean the insurer is refusing to defend. It means the insurer has identified potential coverage issues that could affect its indemnity obligation.

Common ROR triggers in contractors professional liability claims:

- The claim arguably falls outside the policy's definition of "professional services"
- The alleged error may have occurred before the policy's retroactive date
- The insured may have had prior knowledge of the circumstances before the policy inception
- The claim may implicate an exclusion (intentional acts, bodily injury, known prior claims)
- The claim may involve coverage the insurer disputes applies to the specific facts

Upon receiving an ROR letter, the GC should take three immediate steps. First, read it in its entirety and identify which specific policy provisions the insurer has identified as potential coverage issues. Second, engage independent coverage counsel — separate from the panel defense counsel the insurer provides — to evaluate whether the insurer's reserved positions are supportable under the policy language and applicable state law. Third, document all communications with the insurer going forward.

An ROR letter does not necessarily mean coverage will ultimately be denied. Many claims that begin under an ROR are eventually resolved with full indemnification once the facts clarify that the reserved coverage issues do not apply. But GCs who receive an ROR letter and proceed without independent coverage review are trusting the insurer's coverage analysis without verification.

## Panel Counsel vs. Independent Counsel

Most E&O insurers maintain a panel of defense attorneys — experienced insurance defense firms pre-approved by the insurer to handle professional liability claims. When a covered claim is filed, the insurer typically assigns panel counsel to defend.

In a straightforward coverage situation — no ROR, no coverage dispute — panel counsel is generally competent and experienced. They handle E&O matters routinely and know the insurer's expectations.

When an ROR has been issued, the dynamic changes. Panel counsel is retained by and paid by the insurer. If the insurer's coverage position and the insured's interests diverge — which is precisely the circumstance that generates an ROR — panel counsel faces an inherent conflict. Many states have statutory or case law provisions that entitle the insured to select independent counsel at the insurer's expense when a conflict of interest arises from an ROR. This is sometimes called "Cumis counsel" after the California case that established the principle, though the doctrine exists in various forms across multiple jurisdictions.

GCs who receive an ROR letter should immediately ask independent coverage counsel whether they are entitled to independent defense counsel at the insurer's expense. The insurer will not volunteer this information.

## Late Notice: The Pitfall That Costs Coverage

Professional liability claims often develop slowly. An owner's complaint about rework morphs over months into a formal demand. A subcontractor dispute implicates the GC's coordination decisions. A post-project audit surfaces a budget overrun traced to a cost estimation error.

GCs who wait to report these developing circumstances until a formal claim is filed risk running afoul of the policy's notice provisions. Most policies require reporting not just of formal claims but of "circumstances that might reasonably be expected to give rise to a claim." Once a GC is aware that a professional error occurred and that the client has suffered damages, the reporting obligation is likely triggered — even if no demand has been made.

Late notice that prejudices the insurer's ability to investigate or defend the claim can result in a coverage denial. "Prejudice" is evaluated differently under different state laws, but the safe approach is unambiguous: report potential claims promptly. Call your broker. Document the notice. Do not wait for the lawsuit.

## The Cooperation Clause

Every professional liability policy contains a cooperation clause requiring the insured to cooperate fully with the insurer in investigating and defending the claim. This includes providing documents and records promptly, appearing for depositions, attending trial if required, and not taking unilateral actions — including communicating directly with the claimant about the claim — that could affect the defense.

Cooperation clause violations can void coverage entirely. GCs who receive a demand, discuss settlement informally with the owner without insurer involvement, and then later try to invoke coverage may find the insurer arguing that the unauthorized communications prejudiced its defense.

The cooperation clause also has a practical implication for the early stages of a claim: resist the instinct to respond directly to the claimant before the insurer is involved. Even a well-meaning response to an owner's complaint can be used against the GC in subsequent litigation and can complicate the insurer's defense posture.

## Settlement Authority

Professional liability policies typically require insurer consent before the insured can settle a claim. Settlement without insurer consent — including admissions of liability, consent judgments, or agreed damages payments — can void the policy's indemnification obligation for that claim.

Conversely, the insurer's right to settle is not unlimited. Most policies contain a "consent to settle" provision giving the insured the right to veto a settlement. But that consent right typically comes with a consequence: if the insured refuses a reasonable settlement within limits and the ultimate judgment exceeds what the settlement would have been, the insurer's obligation may be capped at the settlement figure it proposed.

Understanding your policy's settlement provisions before a claim is active is essential. The negotiation over settlement authority often occurs under time pressure, and GCs who have not read their policy are at a disadvantage in those conversations.

---

A contractors professional liability policy is a contract. Its value in a claim is determined as much by how the insured manages the claim process as by the underlying policy terms. Prompt notice, understanding the defense cost structure, responding appropriately to reservation of rights, and maintaining the cooperation the policy requires are not administrative details — they are the difference between coverage and catastrophic exposure.
